\section{Research Problem and Design Alternatives}
\begin{frame}{Why Service-Level Mechanisms Are Needed}
Networked applications need mechanisms for service discovery, invocation authorization, and application-data exchange.
\vspace{3mm}
\begin{itemize}
\item Some operations discover a currently willing Provider; others must retain an already selected Provider.
\item Applications must associate permissions, execution state and exchanged data with the requested operation.
\item Changes in service availability, resources and connectivity add further challenges.
\end{itemize}
\vspace{3mm}\bluebox{Research goal: a reusable service protocol. UAV and inference applications provide validation cases, not limits on the framework's intended scope.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Research Gap and Two Research Scopes}
\begin{block}{Foundation: evaluate discovery and authorization}
The initial prototype discovers candidates and selects one Provider whose positive ACK passes validation. Discovery confidentiality, service permissions and management costs matter even without collaboration.
\end{block}
\begin{block}{Extension: coordinate complementary roles}
Some tasks need several Providers with different responsibilities. Separate calls do not define a shared role assignment or enforce dependencies between them.
\end{block}
\vspace{3mm}\bluebox{Research gap: existing invocation mechanisms do not by themselves bind runtime Provider assignments to named-Data dependencies and recovery. Plan-commit support is partial [28]; the proposed research completes these rules and tests their reuse and cost.}
\end{frame}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Research Scope: From Application Needs to Checks}
\small
Follow the invocation lifecycle, informed by invocation [9,10] and authorization [12] mechanisms.

\vspace{2mm}\bluebox{These lifecycle checks motivate the three research questions introduced on the next slide.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three Research Questions}
\small
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1:} When does ABE-backed authorization offer a favorable confidentiality--management-cost tradeoff over a role-constrained alternative inspired by DNMP [12] for protected Provider discovery and invocation, as permission scale, overlap, membership changes and withdrawals vary?
\item \textbf{RQ2:} After ACK collection closes, how can single-Provider invocation be extended so runtime-selected Providers receive explicit roles and named dependency scopes, and accept producer- or cache-retrieved Data only when it matches the committed execution context?
\item \textbf{RQ3:} Under what workload, network and failure conditions do ABE-backed discovery, role/dependency admission and bounded recovery improve completion and latency, and when do their costs outweigh the benefits?
\end{itemize}
\vspace{3mm}\bluebox{RQ1 studies invocation; RQ2 studies collaboration. RQ3 evaluates their benefits and costs, not another protocol.}
\end{frame}

\section{Invocation Foundation}
\begin{frame}{Protocol Roles and Trust Bootstrap}
\begin{itemize}
\item User: requests a service and selects a Provider; the extension also assigns collaboration roles.
\item Provider: offers and executes a service. A node may play both roles.
\item Controller: administers identities and service permissions approved by the organization.
\item Participants use provisioned trust anchors and validation rules for certificates and Controller-signed authorization data.
\end{itemize}
\vspace{3mm}\bluebox{Enrollment establishes identities and service permissions. The User then discovers which authorized Providers are currently willing and selects participants for the task.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Discovery Descriptor versus Post-Selection Input}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\column{.48\linewidth}
\begin{block}{Discovery descriptor}
Needed to decide whether to participate: area, deadline, sensor/model and resource requirements.
\end{block}
\column{.48\linewidth}
\begin{block}{Application input}
Needed to execute the selected task: images, detailed processing parameters, or protected input references.
Input and results use request-scoped symmetric keys. Selection delivers key material encrypted for the selected Provider; the requesting User retains the keys.
\end{block}
\end{columns}
\vspace{4mm}\bluebox{The boundary follows necessity. A location needed to assess coverage belongs in the protected descriptor; an image needed only for processing can wait.}
\end{frame}

\section{RQ1: Discovery and Service Authorization}
\begin{frame}{Three Security Requirements; One Additional Benefit}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Keep discovery descriptors confidential from unauthorized readers before selecting a Provider.
\item Authenticate participants, verify service permissions, and reject replayed invocation messages.
\item Grant and withdraw service permissions during operation.
\end{enumerate}
\vspace{3mm}\bluebox{Additional benefit: aggregate service permissions in a decryption key (DKEY) while retaining identity-signing credentials. This is a management property, not a separate security claim.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reason 1: Confidential Discovery Before Selection}
\begin{itemize}
\item The User knows the service, but not yet the currently willing Provider identities.
\item An observation area and deadline can reveal intended activity even without the full application input.
\item The User encrypts descriptor Content under the service-authorized ABE policy, without listing current Provider identities. [19]
\end{itemize}
\vspace{3mm}\bluebox{Confidential from parties lacking the required decryption capability. Authorized but unselected Providers can read it; names and traffic patterns remain visible.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reason 2: Reuse Attribute-Based Service Permissions}
\begin{itemize}
\item NDNSF uses ABE to keep discovery descriptors non-public before selection. [19]
\item NDNSF reuses the attribute authority to manage Provider and User service permissions. [28]
\item RequestNonce and ProviderChallenge checks use these permissions during invocation.
\end{itemize}
\vspace{3mm}\bluebox{ABE discovery plus role-based invocation is also possible. NDNSF avoids adding role signing credentials solely for service membership; signature validation and request-state checks remain necessary. User DKEYs and challenge processing still add cost.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How NDNSF Checks Service Permissions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Provider permission: recover RequestNonce from ABE-protected Request content and return it in ACK Data. The User compares it with RequestNonce stored for this request.
\item User permission: recover ProviderChallenge from ABE-protected ACK content and construct Selection Data. The Provider validates Selection using its stored ProviderChallenge.
\end{enumerate}
\vspace{3mm}\bluebox{Participants also check current service permissions. Challenge checks establish access to protected values, not exclusive key possession. RequestNonce is an application value, distinct from the Interest Nonce.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Identity and Replay Checks Have Different Jobs}
\begin{block}{Identity authentication}
Validate Data signatures and certificate chains under trust rules. An accepted signing identity alone does not establish service permission.
\end{block}
\begin{block}{Replay protection}
Match messages to an active request and its state. Record whether ProviderChallenge has already been used to accept Selection for execution; duplicate Selection must not start another execution.
\end{block}
\bluebox{Retain or safely invalidate request state, including across restart. Encryption or a challenge value alone does not prevent replay.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{DNMP: Trust-Schema Command Authorization}
\small
Distributed Network Measurement Protocol (DNMP) [12] authorizes measurement commands:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The Network Key anchors role keys, device keys, and the schema.
\item The client uses target, probe type, arguments, and user role to find an allowed signing key; among matching keys, it chooses the least privileged.
\item The receiving Network Observer Daemon validates the publication under the schema before dispatching the probe.
\end{enumerate}
\vspace{2mm}\bluebox{DNMP also uses publication lifetimes and retained state against replay, assuming loosely synchronized clocks. These are concrete command-authorization mechanisms, not just identity checks.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{DNMP-Inspired Role Authorization versus NDNSF}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{p{.27\linewidth}XX}
\toprule Design & How execution is authorized & Discovery protection\\\midrule
DNMP-inspired [12] & Role-constrained signer plus request state & Same ABE descriptor (controlled comparison)\\\midrule
NDNSF ABE-backed & Signature, challenges, permission and request-state checks & Service-level ABE\\\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{3mm}\bluebox{Both designs retain the same ABE-protected discovery, Selection, current-permission and replay checks. This adapts DNMP's role-constrained signing idea; it does not reproduce DNMP. Group encryption is separate.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RQ1: Evaluate Authorization Design Tradeoffs}
\small
Model: one Controller domain, one ABE generation; $n$ Users, $k$ services/User,
and $r$ role certificates/User. Additional objects only.
\par\vspace{3mm}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{p{.53\linewidth}X}
\toprule Authorization arrangement & Additional objects for $n$ Users\\\midrule
Per-service roles ($r=k$) & $nr=nk$: $O(nk)$ certificates\\\midrule
One composite role per User & $nr=n$: $O(n)$ certificates\\\midrule

\end{tabularx}
\par\vspace{2mm}For fixed $k$, $O(nk)=O(n)$. 500 versus 100 is inventory only—not an asymptotic or total-cost advantage.
\par\vspace{3mm}\bluebox{Separate policy size, cryptographic work, bytes, setup, invocation, grants, withdrawals and maintenance.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Runtime Permission Grants and Withdrawals}
\begin{itemize}
\item Both operations update Controller-signed permission status. [28]
\item Ordinary grant: update the target's complete policy; it retrieves a replacement DKEY. Public parameters stay unchanged.
\item Withdrawal: rotate the Controller's ABE master key and public parameters; remaining members obtain DKEYs for their remaining permissions.
\end{itemize}
\vspace{3mm}\bluebox{The master key stays with the Controller. Rotation is Controller-wide. Participants must retrieve and validate updates before applying them; disconnected nodes cannot do so immediately.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Proposed Permission-Update Path: Discovery, Validation and Application}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Revised design: the Controller publishes new public parameters as signed Data; Sync announces availability.
\item Participants retrieve and validate parameters and permission status, then refresh DKEYs as required.
\item Grants also require status discovery, even when public parameters do not change.
\end{enumerate}
\vspace{2mm}\bluebox{Status retrieval and rotation are implemented; Sync announcements are planned for end-to-end validation. Evaluation will measure propagation, rejection delay and rekeying traffic. Previously disclosed keys and plaintext remain accessible to their holders.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Threat Model: What Is Trusted?}
\begin{itemize}
\item Trusted: Controller authorization and the User's planning decision.
\item Untrusted delivery: an object may arrive through a producer, forwarder, cache, or repository and still requires validation.
\item Adversarial cases to test: wrong identities, permissions, challenges, request state, roles, dependencies, or execution identities.
\item Not established by signatures/ABE: honest resource reports, correct inference, truthful images, or resistance to authorized key sharing.
\end{itemize}
\bluebox{Scope: trusted Controller and User, Content confidentiality, and withdrawal after validated updates. Malicious-coordinator agreement and traffic-analysis resistance are outside this scope.}
\end{frame}

\section{RQ2: Runtime Roles and Named Dependencies}
\begin{frame}{RQ2 Extension: From Positive ACKs to a Role Plan}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Application supplies roles, dependencies, constraints and deadline.
\item The User closes ACK collection and freezes eligible, validated positive ACKs.
\item A replaceable planning strategy assigns Providers; the User signs the resulting plan.
\item Providers validate assignments, permitted inputs and resource readiness.
\end{enumerate}
\vspace{3mm}\bluebox{The role template is defined before discovery; concrete signer and name constraints are filled from validated ACKs. Current plan-commit checks are partial [28]; dependency and recovery contracts remain to be validated.}
\end{frame}

\small
Proposed task: User assigns UAV A to view 1, UAV B to view 2, and Provider C to analysis.
\begin{center}

\vspace{2mm}\bluebox{A valid signature and matching request ID do not let B's image fill A's view-1 role. C must check the committed assignment and declared edge before accepting it.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Proposed Plan-to-Data Admission Checks}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Validate the Data signature using the appropriate trust rules.
\item Check active execution and deadline; identify the permitted dependency.
\item For new Data, match request/plan/execution and assigned producer; for historical Data, check explicit reuse permission.
\item Check that the object matches the declared edge, permitted name, data format and key scope.
\item Require complete validated input before the dependent computation starts.
\end{enumerate}
\vspace{3mm}\bluebox{The proposed rules apply equally to Data retrieved from producers, caches and repositories. End-to-end tests will check valid inputs and rejection of wrong-role, wrong-edge and outdated-execution objects.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Collaboration Recovery Needs Explicit State}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{p{.3\linewidth}X}
\toprule Event & Required response\\\midrule
Late ACK & Do not silently change the committed plan\\\midrule
Duplicate Selection & Check prior authorization; do not repeat side effects\\\midrule
Incomplete dependency & Wait, recover, or fail within deadline; partial data is not sufficient\\\midrule
Provider replacement & Authorize new assignment; reject old actions; define permitted reuse\\\midrule
Cancellation & Reject late messages after cancellation\\\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\vspace{3mm}\bluebox{A replacement plan may reuse one old artifact under its original Name and signature; it does not reactivate the old execution. Application checks still decide usability.}
\end{frame}

\section{Backup and References}
\begin{frame}{Backup: Conditional Permission Aggregation}
\begin{itemize}
\item Within one Controller domain, combine several service permissions in one entity's DKEY; retain identity-signing credentials. [19,28]
\item DNMP represents command authority through role keys and schema rules. One role can permit several commands. [12]
\item Where equivalent permissions require distinct role credentials, NDNSF avoids maintaining those extra signing credentials.
\end{itemize}
\vspace{3mm}\bluebox{Fewer independently managed credentials does not imply fixed DKEY size or lower total cost. Policy size, key distribution and updates still matter.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup: When Alternatives May Be Preferable}
\begin{itemize}
\item Known Providers and simple rules: signed invocation plus recipient encryption may be sufficient.
\item A small, stable receiver group: ordinary group-key management may be attractive.
\item Rich command-argument restrictions: DNMP-style rules make those constraints explicit. [12]
\item Many changing entitlements: measure both policy/key updates and credential/rule updates before claiming a management advantage.
\end{itemize}
\vspace{3mm}\bluebox{Compare complete combinations of authorization and encryption. ABE-backed authorization is one design choice, not a universally superior solution.}
\end{frame}
