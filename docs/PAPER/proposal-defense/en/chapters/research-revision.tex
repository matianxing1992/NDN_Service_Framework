\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}
\section{Service Discovery and Authorized Invocation}
Named Data Networking (NDN) supports name-based Data retrieval and data-centric security~\cite{ndn,ndnpacket}. Applications can use a trust schema to specify which signing keys are authorized to sign which Data and guide Data validation~\cite{trustschema}. Sync announces dataset changes so applications can retrieve available Data~\cite{li2018brief,moll2022sok}. These building blocks do not, by themselves, select a service executor or guarantee completion before a deadline; invocation authorization requires application-specific rules and checks.

A requester may know the required service without knowing which authorized Providers are currently reachable, capable, and willing to execute it. Even when one Provider suffices, candidate discovery, authorization, selection, and result retrieval remain application responsibilities. NFN, NFaaS, RICE, and NSC support named computation or invocation~\cite{nfn,nfaas,rice,nsc}. The Distributed Network Measurement Protocol (DNMP) provides a concrete example of Trust-Schema-based command authorization~\cite{dnmp}. These NDN systems provide the computation, invocation, and authorization precedents against which this proposal examines NDNSF's design choices.

\section{The Initial NDNSF Foundation}
The initial NDNSF prototype selects one Provider from multiple candidates for each invocation. The User publishes Request Data, collects acknowledgment (ACK) Data, and publishes Selection Data for the chosen Provider, which executes and publishes Response Data. Candidates offer alternatives for the same invocation; the initial prototype does not assign them to different, interdependent roles.

Discovery and authorization remain research subjects independently of collaboration. A discovery descriptor supplies feasibility information, such as deadlines and resource requirements; application input supplies execution material. In the evaluated design, attribute-based encryption (ABE) protects descriptors, while protected-challenge verification, signatures under trust rules, and request-state checks govern authorization. Authorized but unselected candidates can read descriptors. The study also evaluates input/result protection, permission grants and withdrawals, and management costs.

\section{Extending Invocation to Multi-Provider Collaboration}
Some tasks require complementary services from different Providers. Independently invoking each service does not by itself specify which Provider fills each role or which intermediate objects a dependent service may accept. This proposal investigates an extension that makes those assignments and dependencies explicit while retaining the invocation foundation.

For example, nearby unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) could collect different views of a ground area for analysis by a compute Provider. The extension would assign sensing and processing roles and check each image against its declared dependency. Area and deadline information may be needed before ACK to assess feasibility; processing images can be disclosed through Selection. Descriptor encryption limits reading to authorized candidates, not exposure of names or traffic patterns. This case motivates the extension; recognition algorithms remain an application choice.

\section{Thesis and Research Questions}
The thesis to be evaluated is that named, verifiable objects can support runtime service discovery and authorized single-Provider invocation, and that explicit role and dependency rules can extend this foundation to multi-Provider tasks. The study evaluates the discovery/authorization design and the collaboration extension separately. Benefits must justify their discovery, validation, communication, and key-management costs under the tested conditions.

\begin{enumerate}[label=RQ\arabic*.,leftmargin=*]
\item How can NDNSF discover willing Providers while keeping discovery descriptors confidential from unauthorized readers and checking service permissions before execution, and at what cost relative to complete alternative designs?
\item How can the single-Provider invocation foundation be extended so Providers selected at runtime follow task roles and named dependencies, whether intermediate Data is retrieved from producers or caches?
\item Under what workload and network conditions do these mechanisms improve completion, latency, or execution efficiency, and when do their costs outweigh their benefits?
\end{enumerate}

RQ1 evaluates the invocation foundation, including a DNMP-inspired role-authorization alternative paired with content encryption and adapted to the NDNSF workflow. RQ2 investigates the collaboration extension through UAV and distributed-inference dependencies; RQ3 measures both scopes and their applicable failure behavior separately.

\section{Contribution and Evidence Boundary}
The intended contributions are an evaluated discovery/authorization design, a collaboration protocol enforcing runtime role assignments through named-dependency checks, and evidence of their benefits and costs. These build on established NDN, encryption, and computation mechanisms.

Existing invocation experiments do not validate a multi-role plan. The extension adds role and dependency rules without replacing existing request-ID isolation. Later implementation work is not attributed to the initial prototype; collaboration end-to-end evaluation remains incomplete. Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} separates available findings from required evidence.

\chapter{Related Work and Design Alternatives}
\label{chap:related-work}
\section{Named Computation, Invocation, and Composition}
NFN studies named computation and expression evaluation; NFaaS studies execution placement in a named-function environment~\cite{nfn,nfaas}. RICE provides an invocation handshake with support for authentication and security information~\cite{rice}. NSC specifies signed invocation Interests validated using an application-provided Trust Schema, and discusses protected input and result exchange~\cite{nsc}. These designs establish relevant invocation and authorization mechanisms. Experimental comparisons below refer to the tested NSC configuration, not every feature of its full design.

For the collaboration extension, the question is how a request gathers current willingness and feasibility information before assigning execution roles, and how that assignment constrains intermediate-object use. Long-term service permission does not identify the Provider assigned to a particular predecessor role. Neither a request ID nor a valid signature alone expresses that assignment. The proposed contribution is the enforced connection between runtime assignment and object use, rather than the manifest format itself. Service-composition work such as CaSCON~\cite{cascon} is a relevant comparator; the research will examine assignment timing, dependency validation, and recovery semantics alongside workflow-security and capability-based designs. Novelty beyond this proposed scope remains to be established by that comparison.

\section{Comparing Authorization Designs under the Same Requirements}
\subsection{DNMP as a Concrete Trust-Schema Design}
DNMP uses role-constrained
signing to authorize measurement commands~\cite{dnmp}. Its Network Key anchors
the signing hierarchy for role keys, device keys, and the distributed schema.
Given \texttt{target}, \texttt{probeType}, \texttt{probeArgs}, and the user's
\texttt{roleType}, the client finds a schema-permitted signing key among the
user's available keys. It rejects a missing match and selects the least
privileged key if several match. The receiving Network Observer Daemon (NOD)
dispatches a probe only after publication validation under the schema.
Thus, this is command authorization, not merely verification of an arbitrary
valid signature. DNMP also bounds publication lifetimes and retains recent
publication state for replay protection, assuming loosely synchronized clocks.

\subsection{Adapting the Alternative to NDNSF}
\label{sec:dnmp-alternative}
For comparison, we propose retaining the NDNSF Request--ACK--Selection--Response
workflow while replacing ABE-backed invocation checks with role-constrained
signing rules. The alternative must authorize both User invocation and Provider
participation, match messages to active requests, and permit execution only
after Selection. These adaptations are not features attributed to DNMP itself.
Both designs require descriptor confidentiality before selection, recipient-scoped
input/result protection afterward, and checks for withdrawn authority.

Role granularity is policy-defined: a role can cover multiple actions, and credentials can be reused where the rules permit. DNMP is therefore a concrete authorization precedent, not evidence that every service needs a new signing certificate.

This comparison concerns complete designs, not measured DNMP-versus-NDNSF performance. The invocation-state and encryption choices must be implemented and evaluated under matched requirements before claiming a cost advantage.

Recipient encryption protects selected exchanges; service-group encryption or ABE can protect discovery before participants are selected. Trust Schema alone does not provide confidentiality, but it can be combined with either mechanism.

The proposed alternative requires current revocation-status checks or certificate expiry. Deleting a certificate from a repository does not invalidate cached copies. We do not attribute NDNSF's runtime revocation protocol to DNMP.

\section{Protected Discovery and Permission Management}
NDNSF uses ABE for two related purposes: protecting discovery descriptors before Provider selection and managing service permissions independently of identity-signing credentials. The User publishes encrypted descriptors for shared retrieval, while the Controller issues policy keys defining decryption access. The enhanced NAC-ABE library provides KP-ABE support~\cite{dulal2023nacabe}. NDNSF connects these permissions to invocation by checking protected challenge values, message signatures under trust rules, and current authorization/request state. Content encryption supplies confidentiality; the combined checks govern participation and execution. Section~\ref{sec:abe-rationale} explains the reuse of permission management and its additional costs.

The comparison must include group-key or broadcast-encryption alternatives where appropriate. A stable, small group can make conventional group encryption attractive; known Providers and simple permissions can favor signed invocation with recipient encryption. ABE becomes attractive when recipients are identified by service entitlement before their current identities are known, and multiple entitlements should remain independent of identity-signing credentials. Policy size, membership churn, encryption cost, and authority availability bound this argument.

\section{Participants, Trust, and Exclusions}
These are protocol roles, not necessarily separate devices: one node may be both a User and a Provider. The managed organization registers identities and approves service permissions; the Controller administers that policy. Participants require a provisioned trust anchor and validation rules for identity certificates and Controller material. Not knowing which candidates will respond to this invocation does not mean that the authorization authority has never enrolled those entities. Billing, arbitrary business transactions, globally optimal scheduling, and protection against all denial-of-service traffic are outside the claimed scope.
The User requests a service and selects its Provider; in the collaboration extension, it also coordinates role assignments. Providers offer and execute services. A Controller administers authorization material and signed status. Forwarders and caches can deliver Data without being trusted to authorize execution. The model trusts the Controller and the User's planning decision; it does not claim consensus against a malicious coordinator. An attacker may observe visible names and traffic, inject invalid objects, replay previously valid messages, or operate without the needed service permission. Credential compromise and intentional sharing by an authorized principal require additional controls and are not prevented by ABE alone.

Authorization is permission to perform an operation, authentication establishes the signing identity under trust rules, and confidentiality limits who can recover Content. Valid credentials do not prove honest load reports, correct inference, or truthful sensor observations. Signed status must be obtained and validated before it can affect local authorization; a disconnected participant cannot be assumed to learn revocation immediately. Deadline/expiry checks require an explicit clock policy. Tests must report the allowed skew and failure behavior rather than silently assume synchronized clocks.

\section{Authorization and Confidentiality}
\input{\proposalroot/en/chapters/authorization-rationale}

Discovery confidentiality applies to descriptor Content against parties lacking the required ABE decryption capability. It does not conceal the descriptor from other authorized candidates or provide traffic-analysis resistance. In the request-scoped protection design, the User encrypts application input with a symmetric key and delivers the necessary key material through Selection, encrypted for the chosen Provider's authenticated public key. The response key is shared by the requesting User and selected Provider. Other Users cannot decrypt the protected result under the key-secrecy assumptions; the Provider necessarily knows the plaintext it produces. These are the protection requirements for the evaluated design, rather than claims about every historical implementation path.

\section{Proposed Extension: From ACKs to a Collaboration Plan}
The extension changes the selection problem from choosing one executor to assigning complementary task roles. The application supplies a role/dependency template, required services, feasibility constraints, and deadline. The template may be written manually; automatic generation is not necessary to establish the framework contribution. A replaceable planning policy selects among eligible positive ACKs after the collection window closes. Closing the window freezes the candidates observed for that decision, not all possible Providers in the network. ACK claims can become stale, so a selected Provider still performs execution-time admission and may reject work it can no longer execute.

In the proposed design, the User signs a Plan Manifest containing the request identity, accepted candidate snapshot, exact role-to-Provider assignments, permitted named dependency edges, authorization/key scope, and execution identity. Its digest is computed over a canonical plan body that excludes the digest field itself. Providers validate that signed plan before deriving local dependency checks; a long-term certificate does not specify this task's assignments. The User must record the committed plan and reject a different plan for the same execution identity. This is a local state-machine rule under the trusted-User model, not a consequence of signing alone or protection against a Byzantine User signing two plans.

\section{Proposed Dependency Admission and Execution State}
Under the proposed acceptance rules, a downstream Provider checks that the execution is active and within its deadline before accepting a dependency. The Data signature must validate under the applicable trust rules; the request, plan, and execution identity must match the assignment; the signer must be the assigned predecessor; and the name, data format, and key scope must match a declared edge. Required segments must validate, and incomplete or malformed objects cannot satisfy an edge. The same checks apply to producer-, cache-, and repository-fetched Data. The plan references objects in their actual producers' authorized namespaces rather than republishing them under a different producer identity.

These are proposed acceptance conditions, not a claim that every production path is qualified. Network re-retrieval is not identical to repeating computation. If a task is reassigned, cancellation, fencing, and result-reuse rules must be explicit. An already computed image may remain useful when the new plan explicitly authorizes its reuse; an old object is not automatically invalid solely because it is cached. Application-defined compensation can limit side effects but does not provide distributed atomic transactions or exactly-once physical action.

The authorization design and comparative evaluation are intended to answer RQ1; the proposed plan/state rules and their tests are intended to answer RQ2. Neither question is answered by a design description alone. Existing invocation evidence does not establish collaboration correctness; wrong-role, wrong-edge, replay, cancellation, and direct/cache tests remain separate evaluation obligations.

\section{Conditional Correctness Argument}
Assume uncompromised signing keys, correctly enforced trust/authorization checks, and Providers that obey the specified state machine. Selection-before-execution follows only if every handler-start path checks an active assignment and unspent authorization state. Dependency integrity follows only if every input path, including a cache hit, checks the assigned producer and declared edge before satisfying the dependency. Replacement isolation additionally requires old workers and late callbacks to lose authority under the new execution identity. Restart recovery must preserve or safely invalidate the relevant one-time state; signature validity and cache FreshnessPeriod alone are insufficient.

These are conditional invariants and implementation proof obligations, not a cryptographic proof of a complete system. Tests should inject a wrong-role object within the same request, an undeclared edge from a correctly signed producer, and a late callback after replacement. Each isolates a property that a request ID alone cannot express. Application result correctness and physical side effects remain outside this argument.

\chapter{Application-Level Validation}
\label{chap:applications}
\section{Validation Strategy: Applications Exercise the Framework}
We use NDNSF-UAV and NDNSF-DI to evaluate how NDNSF supports service discovery, authorization, data delivery, and execution management across both single-Provider and multi-Provider workflows. Single-Provider operations remain relevant to RQ1 and RQ3; workflows with complementary roles additionally examine RQ2. The multi-view and distributed-model cases below are representative workloads within these broader applications.

The evaluation maps each application function to the NDNSF path it actually uses and to an observable outcome. An application feature supplies evidence only when that path is exercised and the result is recorded. A local helper call, for example, does not establish remote invocation authorization. Likewise, a working interface or model does not by itself demonstrate framework correctness or performance. The chapter defines validation coverage; Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} distinguishes available measurements from remaining tests.

\section{NDNSF-UAV: Control, Sensing, and Collaboration}
\subsection{Application Structure and Service Workflows}
The UAV application connects an operator's ground station with drone-side service containers. Drone services expose camera operations, telemetry, and mission-related operations through NDNSF. The ground station can also provide an object-detection service, so the same application includes both service consumption and service provision. Application code interprets camera, vehicle, and mission data; NDNSF supplies the reusable invocation and data-delivery mechanisms.

These operations cover different framework requirements. Selecting a currently available sensing Provider exercises discovery and ACK-based selection. Addressing an already chosen vehicle exercises invocation associated with that Provider rather than selecting an arbitrary substitute. Telemetry and mission-status retrieval exercise the association of returned Data with the intended vehicle and active operation. Long-running operations supply cases for cancellation, deadlines, and handling late status. Tests must distinguish rejection of an invalid command from the flight controller's physical execution of an accepted command.

Live video and recordings exercise different data paths. Camera Start/Stop is a service-control operation; the resulting live stream uses the named-data streaming interface. Recording references and complete stored objects require exact object retrieval rather than assumptions about cache persistence. These functions therefore contribute to validating stream lifecycle, ordering and gap handling, protected content delivery, and data-product retrieval. Video codecs, image processing, operator interfaces, and flight safety remain application responsibilities; their use of NDNSF is nevertheless part of framework validation.

\subsection{Representative Collaboration: Multi-View Collection and Analysis}
An operator chooses a target location or area on a map. Request Data carries only the service-protected descriptor needed to assess coverage, viewing feasibility, sensors, and timing. Eligible UAVs return positive ACKs. The proposed workflow assigns complementary viewing roles and a processing role, then delivers execution details and appropriate keys through Selection.

Each sensing UAV signs encrypted ViewImage Data under its own authorized namespace, with observation time and available pose/calibration metadata. The compute Provider retrieves permitted images, performs the specified analysis, and publishes signed Annotation Data. Its encrypted result supplies original image references and permitted image keys to the requester, which retrieves the images and renders annotations. The workflow reuses the original signed images rather than republishing them under another producer's identity.

This case examines role assignments, named dependencies, result reuse, and incomplete-view or processing failures. It does not replace the control, telemetry, video, and recording validation above. GPS alone does not establish camera visibility, depth, or correspondence between views. Improved recognition or localization requires ground truth, a single-view control, and a specified fusion method. MiniNDN~\cite{minindn} and PX4 SITL~\cite{px4} can exercise network behavior and application/autopilot integration respectively; neither establishes real-flight safety.

\section{NDNSF-DI: Inference Lifecycle, State, and Collaboration}
\subsection{Application Structure and Inference Workflows}
NDNSF-DI provides an inference application layer over the framework. It interprets model and task descriptions, coordinates model preparation and execution, and exposes inference results to callers. Its native requester and Provider interfaces include request submission, service registration, result/event handling, and cancellation; conversation-related interfaces also carry continuation state. The presence of an interface is not evidence that every model or recovery configuration has passed end-to-end tests.

A single-Provider inference request exercises service discovery, permission checks, selection, protected input delivery, and result retrieval without requiring model partitioning. Preparing a cold model and reusing a resident model exercise different readiness and artifact-access paths. Model files and recorded artifacts may use managed repository storage; the forwarding Content Store remains opportunistic caching, not guaranteed persistence. DI defines model identity, compatibility, and placement policy, while NDNSF supplies service lifecycle and named-object exchange.

Streamed inference and conversation workflows extend coverage to incremental outputs, cancellation, terminal status, and request-specific state. Model weights, activation tensors, and key/value (K/V) state have distinct lifetimes. The model computes K/V tensors and the inference runtime retains them for later decoding; shared buffers can avoid copying the whole cache at each step~\cite{ortkvcache}. Resident weights do not replace this state. DI owns tokenization and KV compatibility rules; framework validation concerns the authorized request, data exchange, and lifecycle through which the application uses that state. A KV speedup is not automatically an NDNSF speedup.

\subsection{Representative Collaboration: Multi-Provider Inference}
A planner combines a supported model description with runtime ACK capabilities, assigns Providers, and prepares their model fragments. Pipeline partitioning assigns sequential layers or layer groups. Tensor partitioning divides operations or tensors and requires the corresponding collective exchanges. Providers exchange activations or collective inputs over declared dependencies. ONNX~\cite{onnx} describes the computation graph; export alone does not implement cross-Provider collectives or establish numerical correctness. Model partitioning has substantial prior work~\cite{neurosurgeon,ddnn}; the NDNSF question concerns runtime assignment and authorized named-data execution.

Autoregressive generation also uses each generated token in a later decoding step. This temporal dependency differs from forward computation through the layers. The evaluation separates model preparation, prefill, time to first token, subsequent decoding, state storage, and data transfer. Claims remain specific to tested models, precisions, partitions, and devices. A feasible single-Provider baseline is retained; if a model cannot fit one device, distributed feasibility is meaningful but a speedup against a non-running baseline is not.

\chapter{Research Plan, Risks, and Decision Gates}

Select one UAV collection--processing graph and one supported DI partition first. Keep pipeline/tensor/hybrid generality, LLM streaming, and sophisticated recovery only where the implementation and evidence can support them. A negative performance result is an acceptable research finding when its conditions and costs are explained.

\section{Limits That Must Remain Visible}
ABE adds authority, computation, and rekeying costs; disconnected revocation has propagation limits. Candidate ACKs do not prove future capacity or honest reporting. Cache availability does not guarantee task completion. Tests confined to one process do not establish distributed failure behavior. UAV safety and perception accuracy need evidence beyond service-message delivery. Findings from selected hardware and models must be interpreted within those configurations.

\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}
The research builds on NDNSF's initial ability to discover multiple candidates and select one Provider for an invocation. It evaluates that discovery/authorization foundation and investigates an extension for complementary Provider roles and named dependencies. The intended contribution is an evaluated service protocol and design rationale, not a new encryption primitive or the first use of named computation. RQ1 compares ABE-backed authorization with DNMP-inspired role rules paired with content encryption, including their management and withdrawal costs. RQ2 asks how runtime assignments become local rules for accepting named dependencies. RQ3 examines benefits and costs for invocation and collaboration separately. Existing campaigns establish limited invocation findings; the collaboration and cross-application claims still require their declared end-to-end evidence. This separation provides a concrete route from the present proposal to a defensible dissertation.
