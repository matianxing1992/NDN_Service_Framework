\section{Application Motivation and Service-Level Decisions}
Networked applications combine services with different lifetimes and resource needs. A UAV operator issues commands to a particular vehicle, follows changing telemetry, and retrieves video or recorded images. An inference application selects compute resources, obtains model artifacts, and exchanges intermediate results. Across these workflows, developers must associate requests, permissions, execution state, and data objects even when connectivity or available resources change.

A requester may know the required service without knowing which authorized Providers are currently reachable, capable, and willing to execute it. Other operations must retain an already selected Provider, such as stopping a particular camera. The framework must therefore support runtime selection while keeping later commands and results associated with the intended Provider and operation. Here, User, Provider, and Controller are logical protocol roles: a User requests a service, a Provider offers or executes it, and a Controller administers service permissions; one node may hold more than one role. The research question is how a reusable service framework can support these decisions and data exchanges without requiring each application to define its own invocation protocol.

Named Data Networking (NDN) supports name-based Data retrieval and data-centric security~\cite{ndn,ndnpacket}. Names identify Data, guide Interest forwarding, and participate in trust rules; applications can use a trust schema to constrain acceptable signing keys and guide Data validation~\cite{trustschema}. Sync supports discovery of dataset changes so applications can retrieve available Data~\cite{li2018brief,moll2022sok}. NDNSF uses these primitives to discover Providers, authorize invocations, and track service execution. The detailed networking background is developed in Chapter~\ref{chap:related-work}.

Even when one Provider suffices, candidate discovery, authorization, selection, and result retrieval remain application responsibilities. NFN provides a named-function and cached-computation model; NFaaS means Named Function as a Service; RICE means Remote Method Invocation in Information-Centric Networking; and NSC means Named Service Calls~\cite{nfn,nfaas,rice,nsc}. The Distributed Network Measurement Protocol (DNMP) provides a concrete example of Trust-Schema-based command authorization~\cite{dnmp}. These NDN systems provide the computation, invocation, and authorization precedents against which this proposal examines NDNSF's design choices.

\section{The Initial NDNSF Foundation}
The initial NDNSF prototype selects one Provider from multiple candidates for each invocation. The User publishes Request Data, collects acknowledgment (ACK) Data, and publishes Selection Data for the chosen Provider, which executes and publishes Response Data. Candidates offer alternatives for the same invocation; the initial prototype does not assign them to different, interdependent roles.

Discovery and authorization also matter when only one Provider executes a request. A discovery descriptor supplies information needed to assess feasibility, such as deadlines and resource requirements; application input supplies the data needed to perform the task. In the design under evaluation, attribute-based encryption (ABE) limits descriptor access to holders of matching decryption keys. Participants check service permissions using the protected challenge values defined below (RequestNonce and ProviderChallenge), signature validation under trust rules, current permission status, and request state. Authorized but unselected candidates can read descriptors. The study also evaluates input/result protection, permission grants and withdrawals, and management costs.

\section{Extending Invocation to Multi-Provider Collaboration}
Some tasks require complementary services from different Providers. Independently invoking each service does not by itself specify which Provider fills each role or which intermediate objects a dependent service may accept. This proposal investigates an extension that makes those assignments and dependencies explicit while retaining the invocation foundation.

For example, nearby unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) could collect different views of a ground area for analysis by a compute Provider. The extension would assign sensing and processing roles. The compute Provider would check that each image comes from the UAV assigned to the required input. UAVs may need area and deadline information before deciding whether to return a positive ACK. After Selection authorizes the sensing tasks, the UAVs produce images for the assigned compute Provider. Selection supplies execution details and the required access information, not images that have yet to be captured. Descriptor encryption restricts who can read the descriptor; it does not hide names or traffic patterns. This case motivates the extension; recognition algorithms remain an application choice.

\section{Thesis and Research Questions}
The central hypothesis is that a common named-Data protocol can separate candidate discovery from authorized execution and make runtime role assignments enforceable when intermediate objects are used. This would let UAV and inference applications share invocation and dependency-validation mechanisms while retaining their own task semantics. The study tests this hypothesis through message/state invariants, cross-application use of the same mechanisms, and matched measurements of completion and cost. It evaluates single-Provider invocation and multi-Provider collaboration separately.

\begin{enumerate}[label=RQ\arabic*.,leftmargin=*]
\item Under what numbers of users and services, degrees of permission overlap, membership-change rates, and withdrawal frequencies does ABE-backed authorization provide a favorable confidentiality--management-cost tradeoff relative to a DNMP-inspired role-constrained authorization alternative for protected Provider discovery and invocation?
\item How can an ACK-based, single-Provider invocation be extended so that, after candidate collection closes, runtime-selected Providers receive explicit workflow roles and named dependency scopes, and accept intermediate Data---whether retrieved from a producer or a cache---only when it matches the committed producer, name, dependency edge, and execution context?
\item Under what workload, network, and failure conditions do ABE-backed discovery, role/dependency admission, and bounded recovery improve completion and latency, and when do their communication, computation, and credential-management costs outweigh the benefits?
\end{enumerate}

RQ1 evaluates the invocation foundation, including a DNMP-inspired role-authorization alternative paired with content encryption and adapted to the NDNSF workflow. RQ2 investigates the collaboration extension through UAV and distributed-inference dependencies. RQ3 evaluates the utility and cost of the mechanisms studied in RQ1 and RQ2, rather than introducing another protocol; invocation and collaboration are measured separately.

\section{Expected Contributions and Current Foundation}
The intended contribution is an evaluated service framework that combines discovery, authorized invocation, named-object exchange, and execution management, with an extension for complementary Provider roles. The research will determine when its authorization choices are worthwhile, how runtime assignments constrain intermediate inputs and replacement executions, and which mechanisms can be shared across UAV and inference applications. Matched workload measurements will establish benefits and limits, including conditions in which the mechanisms offer no advantage. The contribution concerns these service-level abstractions and their evaluation, not new NDN or encryption primitives.

The existing NDNSF implementation provides an invocation prototype and initial collaboration support. Its current planning path closes candidate collection before validating a plan-commit state (defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:ndnsf}), while preliminary invocation campaigns provide baseline evidence. Building on this foundation, the study will evaluate role and dependency checks within a request, end-to-end correctness, and application benefits. \section{Named Computation, Invocation, and Composition}
Named-computation and invocation systems provide direct precedents for this proposal. NFN and NFaaS study named functions, cached computation, or execution placement~\cite{nfn,nfaas}; RICE provides an invocation handshake with authentication and security information~\cite{rice}; and NSC specifies signed invocation Interests validated with an application-provided Trust Schema~\cite{nsc}. DNMP provides a concrete example of schema-constrained role authorization~\cite{dnmp}. CFN and CaSCON further cover computation graphs, worker placement, or cache-assisted composition~\cite{cfn,cascon}. Therefore, named computation, caching, workflow composition, and role-authorized calls are not claimed as isolated NDNSF novelties.

The narrower research question is how to enforce a late-bound collaboration after the positive and negative ACK collection window closes. The proposed extension assigns eligible Providers to application roles and derives predecessor and dependency checks from that assignment. A valid signature proves provenance and a request identifier provides correlation, but neither alone proves that an intermediate object was produced by the Provider assigned to the required predecessor role or is permitted on the declared dependency edge. The contribution under study is this task-specific connection between runtime assignment and Named Data admission, not a new workflow or manifest format.

The comparison will test this connection under explicit assumptions, including cached retrieval and replacement execution. It will not infer that a prior system lacks a mechanism merely because the mechanism is not summarized in a short description; if an existing system performs the same checks, the claim will be narrowed to the demonstrated integration or trade-off.

\section{Comparing Authorization Designs under the Same Requirements}
\subsection{DNMP as a Concrete Trust-Schema Design}
DNMP uses role-constrained
signing to authorize measurement commands~\cite{dnmp}. Its Network Key is the
trust anchor for the role-key, device-key, and distributed-schema signing hierarchy.
Given \texttt{target}, \texttt{probeType}, \texttt{probeArgs}, and the user's
\texttt{roleType}, the client finds a schema-permitted signing key among the
user's available keys. It rejects a missing match and selects the least
privileged key if several match. The receiving Network Observer Daemon (NOD)
dispatches a probe only after publication validation under the schema.
Thus, this is command authorization, not merely verification of an arbitrary
valid signature. DNMP also bounds publication lifetimes and retains recent
publication state for replay protection, assuming loosely synchronized clocks.

\subsection{Adapting the Alternative to NDNSF}
\label{sec:dnmp-alternative}
For comparison, we propose retaining the NDNSF Request--ACK--Selection--Response
workflow while replacing ABE-backed invocation checks with role-constrained
signing rules. The alternative must authorize both User invocation and Provider
participation, match messages to active requests, and permit execution only
after Selection. These adaptations are not features attributed to DNMP itself.
Both designs require descriptor confidentiality before selection, recipient-scoped
input/result protection afterward, and checks that the required service permissions have not been withdrawn.

Role granularity is policy-defined: a role can cover multiple actions, and credentials can be reused where the rules permit. DNMP is therefore a concrete authorization precedent, not evidence that every service needs a new signing certificate.

This comparison concerns complete designs, not measured DNMP-versus-NDNSF performance. The invocation-state and encryption choices must be implemented and evaluated under matched requirements before claiming a cost advantage.

The confidentiality column requires a separate protection mechanism: Trust-Schema signing rules do not encrypt discovery descriptors.
Recipient encryption protects selected exchanges; service-group encryption or ABE can protect discovery before participants are selected. Trust Schema alone does not provide confidentiality, but it can be combined with either mechanism.

The proposed alternative requires current revocation-status checks or certificate expiry. Deleting a certificate from a repository does not invalidate cached copies. We do not attribute NDNSF's runtime revocation protocol to DNMP.

\section{Protected Discovery and Permission Management}
NDNSF uses NAC-ABE to protect Data associated with named service permissions. In the evaluated design, NAC-ABE uses a key-policy ABE (KP-ABE) construction: ciphertexts carry service attributes, while each decryption key (DKEY) carries the holder's access policy. The enhanced NAC-ABE library supplies the KP-ABE operations required by this construction~\cite{dulal2023nacabe}. This library is an implementation dependency rather than a new NDNSF protocol contribution. With this construction, the User can publish an encrypted discovery descriptor for a service-authorized receiver class without enumerating currently reachable Provider identities, while the Controller issues policy keys defining decryption access. NDNSF connects these permissions to invocation by checking protected challenge values, message signatures under trust rules, and current authorization/request state. Content encryption supplies confidentiality; the combined checks govern participation and execution. Section~\ref{sec:abe-rationale} explains the reuse of permission management and its additional costs.

The comparison must include group-key or broadcast-encryption alternatives where appropriate. A stable, small group can make conventional group encryption attractive; known Providers and simple permissions can favor signed invocation with recipient encryption. ABE becomes attractive when recipients are identified by service entitlement before their current identities are known, and multiple entitlements should remain independent of identity-signing credentials. Policy size, membership churn, encryption cost, and authority availability bound this argument.

\section{Participants, Trust, and Exclusions}
The preceding component description concerns software modules. This section uses User, Provider, and Controller as protocol and trust roles: the User requests a service and selects its Provider; in the collaboration extension, it also coordinates role assignments. Providers offer and execute services. A Controller manages service permissions, key distribution, and signed authorization status. These roles are not necessarily separate devices: one node may be both a User and a Provider.

The managed organization registers identities and approves service permissions; the Controller administers that policy. Participants require a provisioned trust anchor and validation rules for identity certificates and Controller-issued Data. The User may not know which enrolled Providers will respond to a particular invocation. Forwarders and caches can deliver Data without being trusted to authorize execution. The model trusts the Controller and the User's planning decision; it does not provide consensus against a malicious coordinator.

An attacker may observe visible names and traffic, inject invalid objects, replay previously valid messages, or attempt an operation without the needed service permission. Credential compromise and intentional sharing by an authorized principal require additional controls and are not prevented by ABE alone. Billing, arbitrary business transactions, globally optimal scheduling, and protection against all denial-of-service traffic are outside the claimed scope.

Authorization is permission to perform an operation, authentication establishes the signing identity under trust rules, and confidentiality limits who can decrypt protected content. Valid credentials do not prove honest load reports, correct inference, or truthful sensor observations. Signed status must be obtained and validated before it can affect local authorization; a disconnected participant cannot be assumed to learn revocation immediately. Deadline/expiry checks require an explicit clock policy. Tests must report the allowed skew and failure behavior rather than silently assume synchronized clocks.

\section{Authorization and Confidentiality}

Discovery confidentiality applies to descriptor Content against parties lacking the required ABE decryption capability. It does not conceal the descriptor from other authorized candidates or provide traffic-analysis resistance. In the request-scoped protection design, the User encrypts application input with a symmetric key and delivers the necessary key material through Selection, encrypted for the chosen Provider's authenticated public key. The response key is shared by the requesting User and selected Provider. Other Users cannot decrypt the protected result under the key-secrecy assumptions; the Provider necessarily knows the plaintext it produces. These are the protection requirements for the evaluated design, rather than claims about every historical implementation path.

\section{Proposed Extension: From ACKs to a Collaboration Plan}
For this proposal, a candidate snapshot is the canonical, ordered set of validated eligible Provider offers observed when the ACK collection window closes. Each record includes the Provider identity and the signed offer/ACK information used for eligibility, such as its service capability and validity data. The snapshot digest is a cryptographic digest of that canonical encoding. A logical request is identified by its request ID; an execution attempt ID distinguishes one run of that request, including a replacement. The execution identity is the request/attempt pair. The snapshot is immutable for that attempt; a replacement receives a new attempt and must not silently reuse the old assignment.

The current NDNSF implementation already closes candidate collection, checks the assigned snapshot digest, verifies that selected Providers belong to that snapshot, and rejects conflicting plan commits. These mechanisms provide an implementation basis for the collaboration study. The remaining research evaluates dependency acceptance, recovery, and cross-application behavior against the acceptance rules defined below.

The extension changes the selection problem from choosing one executor to assigning complementary task roles. The application supplies a role/dependency template, required services, feasibility constraints, and deadline. The template may be written manually; automatic generation is not necessary to establish the framework contribution. A replaceable planning policy selects among eligible positive ACKs after the collection window closes. Closing the window freezes the candidates observed for that decision, not all possible Providers in the network. ACK claims can become stale, so a selected Provider still performs execution-time admission and may reject work it can no longer execute.

In the proposed design, the User signs a Plan Manifest containing the request identity, candidate-snapshot digest, exact role-to-Provider assignments, permitted named dependency edges, authorization/key scope, and execution identity. Its plan digest is computed over a canonical plan body that excludes the digest field itself. Providers validate that signed plan before deriving local dependency checks; a long-term certificate does not specify this task's assignments. The User must record the committed plan and reject a different plan for the same execution identity. This is a local state-machine rule under the trusted-User model, not a consequence of signing alone or protection against a Byzantine User signing two plans.

The technical difficulty is that the abstract dependency graph can be known before discovery, but its concrete signing identities and object namespaces cannot yet be fixed. The proposed protocol therefore separates an application-supplied template from its post-ACK binding. Each consumer derives its permitted predecessor, object-name constraints, and key scope from the same committed assignment. A later ACK cannot change only one consumer's interpretation. This is stronger than waiting until an arbitrary input is available, but does not establish optimal placement or agreement among malicious planners.

\section{Proposed Dependency Admission and Execution State}
Under the proposed acceptance rules, a downstream Provider checks that the execution is active and within its deadline before accepting a dependency. The Data signature must validate under the applicable trust rules. Newly produced inputs must match the assigned request, plan, execution identity, predecessor, and declared edge; historical inputs instead require the explicit reuse authorization described below. Name, format, and key-scope constraints still apply to both. Required segments must validate, and incomplete or malformed objects cannot satisfy an edge. The same checks apply whether Data is retrieved from a producer, a cache, or a repository. The plan references objects in their actual producers' authorized namespaces rather than republishing them under a different producer identity.

The proposed recovery rules separate retrieving an object again from repeating computation. Once a Provider applies a replacement, old execution messages and callbacks cannot advance the new execution.

An existing artifact is reusable only when the new signed plan explicitly references its exact immutable identity, original producer and provenance, destination dependency, and permitted key scope. The artifact retains its original name and signature; it is not relabeled as newly produced. The consumer checks that explicit reuse authorization instead of requiring the historical artifact to claim the new execution identity. All other old objects remain ineligible.

Application checks must additionally establish that a captured image is still timely or that a tensor/state object is compatible. Thus, an old artifact may remain useful without allowing messages from its old execution to advance the replacement. Application-defined compensation does not provide distributed atomic transactions or exactly-once physical action.

RQ1 evaluates the authorization design and its alternatives. RQ2 tests the plan and state rules through wrong-role and wrong-edge inputs, replay, cancellation, and equivalent validation of Data obtained from producers and caches. These collaboration experiments extend the invocation evidence presented in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}.

\section{Conditional Correctness Argument}
The preceding admission argument is conditional on uncompromised signing keys, correctly enforced trust and authorization checks, and Providers that obey the specified state machine. To permit execution only after Selection, every path that starts a handler must check an active assignment and confirm that the one-time authorization state has not already been used to start execution.

To enforce the declared dependencies, every input path, including retrieval from a cache, must check the dependency edge and the assigned producer. Historical artifacts instead require explicit reuse permission. The artifact reference must identify exact Data, for example by a full name including its implicit digest or a validated segment manifest with content digests.

After replacement, old workers and late callbacks must not advance the new execution. Restart recovery must preserve or safely invalidate the relevant one-time state. A valid signature and cache FreshnessPeriod alone cannot establish these properties.

The study will test these conditional invariants by injecting a wrong-role object within the same request, an undeclared input from a correctly signed producer, and a late callback after replacement. Each case isolates a property that a request ID alone cannot express. The argument concerns protocol acceptance and execution state under the stated assumptions; application-result correctness and physical side effects require separate validation.

\chapter{UAV Application over NDNSF}
\label{chap:uav}
\label{chap:applications}
NDNSF-UAV exercises service discovery, authorization, data delivery, and execution management through control, telemetry, video, recordings, and collaborative sensing. Single-Provider operations examine the invocation foundation; multi-view collection additionally tests complementary roles. Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} specifies the comparisons and measurements.

\section{Application Structure and Service Workflows}
The UAV application connects an operator's ground station with drone-side service containers. Drone services expose camera operations, telemetry, and mission-related operations through NDNSF. The ground station can also provide an object-detection service, so the same application includes both service consumption and service provision. Application code interprets camera, vehicle, and mission data; NDNSF supplies the reusable invocation and data-delivery mechanisms.

These operations cover different framework requirements. Selecting a currently available sensing Provider exercises discovery and ACK-based selection. Addressing an already chosen vehicle exercises invocation associated with that Provider rather than selecting an arbitrary substitute. Telemetry and mission-status retrieval exercise the association of returned Data with the intended vehicle and active operation. Long-running operations supply cases for cancellation, deadlines, and handling late status. Tests must distinguish rejection of an invalid command from the flight controller's physical execution of an accepted command.

Live video and recordings exercise different data paths. Camera Start/Stop is a service-control operation; the resulting live stream uses the named-data streaming interface. Recording references and complete stored objects require exact object retrieval rather than assumptions about cache persistence. These functions therefore contribute to validating stream lifecycle, ordering and gap handling, protected content delivery, and data-product retrieval. Video codecs, image processing, operator interfaces, and flight safety remain application responsibilities; their use of NDNSF is nevertheless part of framework validation.

\section{Representative Collaboration: Multi-View Collection and Analysis}
An operator chooses a target location or area on a map. Request Data carries only the service-protected descriptor needed to assess coverage, viewing feasibility, sensors, and timing. Eligible UAVs return positive ACKs. The proposed workflow assigns complementary viewing roles and a processing role, then delivers execution details and appropriate keys through Selection.

Each sensing UAV signs encrypted ViewImage Data under its own authorized namespace, with observation time and available pose/calibration metadata. The compute Provider retrieves permitted images, performs the specified analysis, and publishes signed Annotation Data. Its encrypted result supplies original image references and permitted image keys to the requester, which retrieves the images and renders annotations. The workflow reuses the original signed images rather than republishing them under another producer's identity.

The Global Positioning System (GPS) alone does not establish camera visibility, depth, or correspondence between views. Improved recognition or localization requires ground truth, a single-view control, and a specified fusion method. MiniNDN~\cite{minindn} and PX4 Software in the Loop (SITL)~\cite{px4} can exercise network behavior and application/autopilot integration respectively; neither establishes real-flight safety.

\chapter{Distributed Inference over NDNSF}
\label{chap:di}
NDNSF-DI is the distributed-inference (DI) application over NDNSF. It exercises discovery, authorization, model and tensor exchange, and execution management through single-Provider inference and collaborative model execution. Its computation and retained state provide a contrasting workload to UAV sensing and external actions. Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} evaluates the shared framework mechanisms and application-specific checks.

\section{Application Structure and Inference Workflows}
NDNSF-DI provides an inference application layer over the framework. It interprets model and task descriptions, coordinates model preparation and execution, and exposes inference results to callers. Its native requester and Provider interfaces include request submission, service registration, result/event handling, and cancellation; conversation-related interfaces also carry continuation state. The presence of an interface is not evidence that every model or recovery configuration has passed end-to-end tests.

Python bindings adapt the native C++ path rather than maintain a second request state machine.

The NDNSF runtime supplies reusable operation, completion, and event-reading mechanisms. DI defines model preparation, placement, tensor contracts, K/V compatibility, and the rules for committing a completed turn to conversation state.

A single-Provider inference request exercises service discovery, permission checks, selection, protected input delivery, and result retrieval without requiring model partitioning. Preparing a cold model and reusing a resident model exercise different readiness and artifact-access paths. Model files and recorded artifacts may use managed repository storage; the forwarding Content Store remains opportunistic caching, not guaranteed persistence. DI defines model identity, compatibility, and placement policy, while NDNSF supplies service lifecycle and named-object exchange.

Streamed inference and conversation workflows extend coverage to incremental outputs, cancellation, terminal status, and request-specific state. Model weights, activation tensors, and key/value (K/V) state have distinct lifetimes. The model computes K/V tensors and the inference runtime retains them for later decoding; shared buffers can avoid copying the whole cache at each step~\cite{ortkvcache}. Resident weights do not replace this state. DI owns tokenization and KV compatibility rules; framework validation concerns the authorized request, data exchange, and lifecycle through which the application uses that state. The evaluation must separate the benefits of local K/V reuse from those of NDNSF's communication and execution mechanisms.

\section{Representative Collaboration: Multi-Provider Inference}
A planner uses the prepared model and validated ACK offers to choose a partition and Provider assignment after candidate collection closes. Selected Providers prepare or reuse their assigned resources after authenticating Selection. Pipeline partitioning assigns sequential layers or layer groups. Tensor partitioning divides operations or tensors and requires the corresponding collective exchanges. Providers exchange activations or collective inputs over declared dependencies. Open Neural Network Exchange (ONNX)~\cite{onnx} describes the computation graph; export alone does not implement cross-Provider collectives or establish numerical correctness. Model partitioning has substantial prior work~\cite{neurosurgeon,ddnn}; the NDNSF question concerns runtime assignment and authorized named-data execution.

Autoregressive generation also uses each generated token in a later decoding step. This temporal dependency differs from forward computation through the layers. The evaluation separates model preparation, prefill, time to first token, subsequent decoding, state storage, and data transfer. Claims remain specific to tested models, precisions, partitions, and devices. A feasible single-Provider baseline is retained; if a model cannot fit one device, distributed feasibility is meaningful but a speedup against a non-running baseline is not.

\section{Cross-Application Validation}
The study uses UAV and DI workflows to examine which discovery, authorization, dependency, and lifecycle rules can be shared, and which decisions require application-specific contracts. \section{Primary Research Scope and Feasibility}
The initial UAV graph will assign two image-collection roles and one analysis role; its matched control uses the same images and recognition algorithm. The initial DI case will target a two-Provider YOLO (You Only Look Once) partition, with every tensor crossing the partition boundary declared as a dependency. Model version, cut, precision, input set, and numerical tolerance will be fixed after graph-compatibility checks and before comparative runs. A feasible unsplit execution supplies the numerical reference and performance control. These are planned configurations, not completed validations.

The minimum scope includes valid completion, invalid-input rejection, and one controlled cancellation/replacement case in each graph. Additional partition strategies, LLM streaming, larger deployments, and real-flight trials are extensions, not prerequisites for these bounded framework findings. Existing control, telemetry, video, and single-Provider inference workflows retain their validation roles. If either core graph cannot be validated, its RQ2 claim remains unsupported and the scope or schedule must be reconsidered with the committee. The analysis will report both beneficial and unfavorable operating conditions.

Validation proceeds from local correctness tests to controlled MiniNDN campaigns, UAV simulation and bench integration, and supported physical devices. Each stage establishes evidence only for the tested environment. Physical-flight experiments require the appropriate approval; simulation alone cannot establish real-flight safety or perception performance.

ABE adds authority, computation, and rekeying costs; disconnected revocation has propagation limits. Candidate ACKs do not prove future capacity or honest reporting. \chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}
This dissertation studies a reusable service framework over NDN. It builds on NDNSF's initial ability to discover multiple candidates and select one Provider for an invocation, evaluates its invocation and data-exchange mechanisms, and extends them to complementary Provider roles and named dependencies.

RQ1 will identify conditions favoring ABE permission reuse or complete role-authorized alternatives as permission overlap, membership, and withdrawal frequency change. RQ2 will determine how assignments made after ACK collection constrain new and explicitly reused named objects without allowing old execution messages to advance their replacements. RQ3 will measure benefits and costs across UAV and DI workflows, and test which checks the framework can share and which require application-specific validation.

These are proposed design and protocol contributions, not a new encryption primitive or the first use of named computation. Existing campaigns establish limited invocation findings; the collaboration and cross-application claims still require their declared end-to-end evidence. The proposal asks the committee to assess the research scope, comparisons, validation criteria, and feasibility. The dissertation will report supported answers and limitations, including conditions in which the proposed mechanisms offer no advantage.
